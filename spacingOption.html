Tight Layout

No intentional gaps between materials. Any leftover width becomes a ripped piece at one or both edges.

Common for tongue-and-groove flooring, hardwood flooring, wainscoting, ceiling planks, wall paneling, cabinet fillers, and other tight-fit installations.

Often used when materials are designed to interlock or visually appear continuous.

Example: 

| board | board | board | rip |

Or centered: 

| rip | board | board | rip |
Fixed Gap Field

Uses a fixed spacing between materials, but no intentional spacing at the start or end edges.

Common for decking, tile layouts, brick courses, pavers, rainscreen siding, fencing, and exterior trim layouts where field spacing must remain consistent.

Leftover width becomes either:

  • a single rip at one edge
  • split evenly across both edges

Example: 

| board | gap | board | gap | board | rip |

Or centered: 

| half rip | board | gap | board | half rip |
Equal Gaps Everywhere

Creates identical spacing at both ends and throughout the field. Every visible gap is equal.

Common for balusters, soffit tails, pergola slats, privacy screens, decorative wall slats, fence pickets, wine racks, shelving dividers, and modern architectural layouts where visual symmetry is the priority.

Typically used when the spacing itself is the visual feature.

Example: 

| gap | board | gap | board | gap |
Fixed Field Gap + Equal Edges

Keeps spacing between materials fixed, while balancing the start and end gaps equally.

Common for decking, tile layouts, brick patterns, pavers, exterior siding reveals, trim layouts, and architectural panel systems where field spacing must remain exact but edge balance improves appearance.

Unlike "Equal Gaps Everywhere", only the edge gaps change size. Interior gaps remain fixed.

Example: 

| edge gap | board | fixed gap | board | edge gap |
Centered Layout / Equal Rips

Centers the layout by splitting leftover material evenly across both sides using ripped pieces.

Common for flooring, tongue-and-groove, wainscoting, ceiling planks, backsplash tile, wall paneling, cabinet layouts, and finish carpentry where avoiding a tiny edge strip improves appearance.

Often used when symmetry is visually important but maintaining full-width pieces throughout the field is preferred.

Example: 

| rip | board | board | board | rip |
Max Fill / Code Layout

Maximizes the number of materials while keeping spacing below a maximum limit.

Common for code-compliant balusters, stair guards, fencing, child-safe railings, livestock fencing, cage dividers, and layouts where spacing regulations or safety rules apply.

Typically prioritizes:

  • maximum quantity
  • smallest safe spacing
  • code compliance

Example: 

| small gap | board | small gap | board | small gap |
